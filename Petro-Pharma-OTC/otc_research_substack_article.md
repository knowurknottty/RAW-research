# The OTC Label Audit Didn't Collapse — It Survived

*Adversarial research notes, August 2026*

---

There is a predictable move people make when a research question sounds awkward, taboo, or a little too far outside approved framing: they dismiss the pattern before they inspect the record.

That move did not survive this audit.

Once the 50-row OTC worksheet was pushed through label-by-label primary-source review — using DailyMed product disclosures as the primary reference, adversarial disconfirmation as the method, and publication-grade wording as the output standard — the underlying pattern was real.

A large spread of ordinary American OTC products did in fact surface petrochemical-linked ingredients at the label level. Sometimes as actives. More often as solvents, preservatives, polymers, coating agents, propellants, or related excipients.

But the research also forced a more disciplined conclusion than a viral talking point would allow. The strongest version was not "every product always contains the same thing." The strongest version was narrower and tougher: these ingredients appear widely and repeatedly across mainstream OTC products, but the exact pattern frequently depends on formulation, dosage form, and manufacturer.

That narrowing is not a retreat. It is what makes the claim survive scrutiny.

---

## What closed cleanly

Some rows were hard to argue with.

The proton-pump inhibitors — omeprazole, esomeprazole magnesium, lansoprazole — repeatedly surfaced the same kinds of enteric-coating polymer systems across multiple labels. That's not a pattern you can explain away as cherry-picking; it's a formulation architecture that's practically required for the mechanism to work in the GI tract.

Polyethylene glycol 3350 (MiraLAX) was obvious by chemical definition. The active ingredient *is* a petroleum-derived polymer.

Adapalene gel (Differin) and minoxidil (Rogaine) were strong label-level examples. Ketotifen fumarate (Zaditor, Alaway) and olopatadine hydrochloride (Pataday) closed cleanly on ophthalmic label evidence, both repeatedly listing benzalkonium chloride — a petrochemical-derived preservative — across multiple product presentations.

---

## What required more honesty

Other rows demanded more care.

Calcium carbonate chewable antacids, loperamide, simethicone infant drops, bisacodyl, sennosides, magnesium hydroxide suspensions, glycerin suppositories, and several topical antifungals did not justify lazy blanket statements. Some surfaced formulations were simpler. Some were materially different by dosage form. Some counterexamples landed too clearly to ignore.

The audit kept them. It would have been easy to bury the cleaner loperamide-only presentation, or the simpler bisacodyl suppository, or the magnesium hydroxide snippet that looked different from the others. But that is not how adversarial research works. If you hide counterexamples, you lose credibility. And more importantly, you lose the actual finding — which is a qualified pattern claim, not a universal one.

---

## Why the qualified version is the stronger version

A claim that says "a broad set of ordinary U.S. OTC products contain petrochemical-linked ingredients at the label level, with strength depending on formulation and dosage form" is immune to the obvious counterattack.

A claim that says "all OTC products are full of petroleum chemicals" collapses on first contact with a simpler formulation.

The first version is what the data support. It is also the version that cannot be laughed off by a fact-checker who finds one clean label.

The honest version survived. That is the part worth publishing.

---

## A note on method

This audit was run under an adversarial standard: no claim was accepted because it was rhetorically useful, no counterexample was hidden because it was inconvenient, and no row was marked positive without label-level evidence. Rows where the retained research record didn't preserve individual label data are marked plainly as unaudited in this artifact rather than filled in with guesswork.
